Part 1: The Basics

What the fee schedule is

The Medicare Physician Fee Schedule (PFS) is the price list Medicare uses to pay for professional services by physicians, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, therapists and other clinicians. Each service has a billing code (a Current Procedural Terminology, or CPT, code), and each code has a payment amount. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) updates the schedule every year.

How a price is built

Every code is scored in relative value units (RVUs) in three categories:

RVU type What it measures
Work The clinician's time, skill and effort
Practice expense (PE) Overhead: staff, supplies, equipment, rent
Malpractice (MP) The cost of professional liability insurance

Each RVU type is adjusted for location with its own Geographic Practice Cost Index (GPCI). The total is then multiplied by one dollar amount, the conversion factor (CF):

Payment = [(Work RVU × Work GPCI) + (PE RVU × PE GPCI) + (MP RVU × MP GPCI)] × Conversion Factor

  • RVUs set the relative value of services (for example, a surgery is worth 20 times an office visit).
  • GPCIs adjust each part for local costs.
  • The conversion factor turns those relative values into dollars.

Exceptions to a single conversion factor

The same conversion factor applies to nearly every service, with two exceptions:

  • Two conversion factors by APM status. Since 2026, the law sets a slightly higher annual update for clinicians who qualify through an advanced alternative payment model (APM), an arrangement in which clinicians take on financial risk for the cost and quality of care. Those qualifying participants have their own conversion factor; everyone else uses the standard one.
  • A separate anesthesia conversion factor. Anesthesia services aren't priced with RVUs. Payment is (base units + time units) × the anesthesia conversion factor. Base units are assigned to each anesthesia code and cover the work apart from time; time units count anesthesia time, one unit per 15 minutes. CMS publishes the anesthesia conversion factor for each locality.

Two prices for most services

Most codes have two PE values:

  • Non-facility: the service is done in a doctor's office, and the practice pays the overhead. The PE value is higher.
  • Facility: the service is done in a hospital, which bills Medicare separately for its overhead. The PE value is lower.

Who pays

Medicare pays 80% of the fee schedule amount. The patient (or their supplemental insurance) pays 20%, after the annual deductible.

Part 2: The Annual Cycle at a Glance

Example: rates for 2027

Step What happens When (2027 example)
1 Codes enter the annual update (new, revised or flagged) 2025 and earlier
2 The RUC recommends RVUs 2025 – early 2026
3 CMS decides the RVUs (work, PE, malpractice, plus location adjustments) Spring 2026
4 PE and malpractice are balanced within their own pools Spring 2026
5 CMS calculates the conversion factor Spring 2026
6 Proposed rule is published, followed by public comment Summer 2026 (about 60 days)
7 Final rule is published, with RVUs revised and Steps 4–5 redone Around November 2026
8 New rates take effect January 1, 2027

Key principle: the conversion factor always comes last, because it depends on the final RVUs.

Part 3: Step by Step

Step 1: Codes enter the annual update

A code goes to the RUC only when it needs a value. There are two ways in.

Way 1: New or revised codes

  • The American Medical Association (AMA) owns the CPT code set. Its CPT Editorial Panel creates and revises codes.
  • Specialty societies and device makers apply for new codes when a new service or technology has no code that fits.
  • A new code has no value yet, and a substantially revised code needs a new one. Both go to the RUC.

Way 2: Flagged ("potentially misvalued") codes

  • These are existing codes whose RVUs may be wrong, usually because the service has become faster or easier over time but kept its old value.
  • Because of budget neutrality (Steps 4–5), an overvalued code takes money from every other code. That's why these codes get looked for.
  • Who flags them:
  • CMS, which is required by law to identify misvalued codes. It uses data screens such as fast billing growth, services moving from hospitals to offices, codes usually billed together, and high-cost codes not reviewed in years.
  • The RUC, through its own review workgroup
  • The public, since anyone can nominate a code to CMS
  • Outside watchdogs, such as the Medicare Payment Advisory Commission (MedPAC), the Government Accountability Office (GAO) and the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) Inspector General

Step 2: The RUC recommends RVUs

The RUC (AMA/Specialty Society Relative Value Scale Update Committee) is a private AMA committee of about 30 members, mostly from specialty societies.

  1. The relevant specialty surveys its physicians about the service: how long it takes and how intense it is.
  2. The specialty presents a recommended value to the RUC.
  3. RUC members from other specialties question it and compare it to similar reference codes.
  4. A recommendation needs a two-thirds vote to pass. It's often lower than what the specialty asked for.
  5. The RUC sends CMS its recommendations for:
  6. Work RVUs
  7. Direct PE inputs, meaning the ingredients of a service (staff minutes, supplies, equipment time) but not their prices

The RUC deals only in RVUs and inputs. It never sets dollar amounts or the conversion factor.

Step 3: CMS decides the RVUs

CMS has the final say. RUC recommendations are advice.

3a. Work RVUs

CMS reviews each recommendation and either accepts it, changes it, or replaces it with its own value. Its main tools:

  • Crosswalk: match the code to an existing code with similar time and complexity, and use that code's value.
  • Time ratio: if the service now takes less time, cut the RVUs in proportion.
  • Survey quality check: if the survey data is too thin or unreliable, keep the existing value or set one some other way.
  • Broad policy adjustments: sometimes CMS adjusts a whole group of codes at once (for example, a percentage reduction for services it believes have become more efficient over time).

CMS explains its reasoning for each code in the proposed rule.

3b. Practice expense (PE) RVUs

PE has two parts:

  • Direct costs (tied to a specific service): CMS takes the RUC-recommended inputs (staff minutes, supplies, equipment) and prices them using its own price data.
  • Indirect costs (rent, front desk, billing, IT): CMS spreads these across codes with a formula based on each specialty's overall cost structure and, in part, on work RVUs. That's why work RVU changes can also shift PE.

Each code gets a facility and a non-facility PE value.

3c. Malpractice (MP) RVUs
  • There's no RUC survey. CMS uses malpractice insurance premium data by specialty.
  • A code's MP RVU reflects the premiums of the specialties that usually perform it.
  • A full recalculation from new premium data happens every few years. In between, new and revised codes get values based on which specialties perform them.
3d. Geographic adjustments (GPCIs)
  • What they are: every Medicare payment area, called a locality, has three GPCIs, one for each RVU type. Each compares local costs with the national average, which is set at 1.00. A PE GPCI of 1.10 means practice costs in that area run about 10% above average.
  • What they do: they raise payments where running a practice costs more and lower them where it costs less. Updates generally move money between localities without changing the national total.
  • Limits set by law:
  • The work GPCI reflects only one-quarter of local differences in physician earnings, which keeps its range narrow.
  • Some areas have minimum values (floors): a work GPCI floor of 1.00 that Congress has extended repeatedly, plus permanent floors for Alaska and for a handful of low-density "frontier" states.
  • CMS must review and update the GPCIs at least every three years.

Step 4: Budget neutrality within the PE and malpractice pools

The rule: RVU changes may not, by themselves, increase total Medicare spending (beyond a small $20 million cushion set by law).

PE and malpractice are each balanced within their own pool. The steps are the same for both:

  1. Take billing counts from a recent complete year, the same counts for every calculation. (For 2027 rates, this is generally 2025 data.)
  2. Target: last year's PE RVUs × those billing counts, summed across all codes.
  3. New total: next year's full list of PE RVUs (every code, changed or not) × the same billing counts, summed.
  4. If the new total is higher than the target, multiply every code's PE RVU by one factor until the two totals match.
Why the billing counts matter

The counts are the same in both years, but they're different for each code. They work as weights.

Code Times billed Old PE RVU New PE RVU
A (office visit) 1,000,000 1.0 1.1
B (rare surgery) 10 5.0 4.9
  • Adding up the RVUs alone: 6.0 before, 6.0 after. It looks like no change.
  • Weighting by billing counts: 1,000,050 before, 1,100,049 after. Spending is about 10% higher.

The increase on Code A is paid a million times. The decrease on Code B saves only ten times.

Who pays for an increase

Example: 10,000 codes on next year's list. 200 got PE increases, and the PE total comes out 1% too high. CMS multiplies all 10,000 codes by about 0.99.

  • The 200 raised codes: still higher than last year, but by a little less than the raw increase.
  • The 9,800 unchanged codes: about 1% lower, even though nothing about them changed.

Budget neutrality controls price changes, not volume. If more services are billed next year, actual spending can still go up.

Step 5: The conversion factor

The goal

Keep total spending level after the year's RVU changes, then apply only the changes Congress has set in law.

Why the conversion factor is needed for budget neutrality
  • PE and malpractice were balanced in Step 4.
  • Work RVUs are not rescaled within their pool. If work RVUs go up overall, the work total is allowed to grow.
  • That extra work spending is offset by lowering the conversion factor, which affects every service.
Part A: Budget-neutrality adjustment
  1. Use the same prior-year billing counts.
  2. Old total: (old work + PE + MP RVUs) × billing counts × old CF, summed across all codes.
  3. New total: (new work + PE + MP RVUs) × the same billing counts × the same old CF, summed.

  4. Adjustment factor = Old total ÷ New total

  5. If RVUs went up overall, the factor is below 1 and the conversion factor goes down.

  6. If RVUs went down overall, the factor is above 1 and the conversion factor goes up.
  7. Since PE and MP are already balanced, this adjustment mostly reflects work RVU changes.
Part B: Changes set by law (not budget neutral)

These are meant to change total spending:

  • The annual update set by law: a small fixed percentage each year.
  • Temporary changes from Congress: Congress sometimes passes a one-time raise for a single year. When it expires, it comes back out of the conversion factor the next year, which looks like a cut.
Two conversion factors, plus anesthesia
  • Because the law sets a slightly higher annual update for qualifying APM participants, CMS calculates two conversion factors: one for those clinicians and one for everyone else. The steps above apply to both.
  • CMS also updates the separate anesthesia conversion factor each year and publishes it for every locality.
Putting it together

New CF = Old CF × budget-neutrality adjustment × annual update set by law (× add or remove any temporary changes from Congress)

Example with toy numbers:

Step Adjustment Conversion factor
Last year's CF — $30.00
Budget neutrality (work RVUs rose overall) −0.5% $29.85
Annual update set by law +0.25% $29.92

The order doesn't matter, since these are all multiplications.

Step 6: Proposed rule and public comment

  • CMS publishes the proposed RVUs for every code, its reasoning for each one, and the proposed conversion factors.
  • There's a public comment period of about 60 days. Specialty societies, hospitals, insurers, MedPAC, members of Congress and others comment.

Step 7: Final rule

  • CMS considers the comments, responds to the main ones in the rule, and makes final changes to the RVUs.
  • Because the RVUs may have changed, CMS redoes Steps 4 and 5. That's why the final conversion factor often differs from the proposed one. (Toy example: proposed $29.92, final $29.90.)
What often changes between proposed and final
  • Values for individual codes, when commenters bring new data or arguments
  • Policy proposals, which can be finalized, modified, delayed or dropped
  • The conversion factor, because Steps 4 and 5 are rerun
What usually stays fixed
  • Updates set by law. CMS can't change them; only Congress can.
  • The budget-neutrality requirement
  • The rule's scope. Under federal rulemaking law, a final rule has to be a "logical outgrowth" of the proposal, so ideas CMS didn't propose usually wait for a later year.
Who's involved
  • CMS writes the rule and answers comments.
  • HHS: the rule is issued under the Department of Health and Human Services, CMS's parent department.
  • The White House Office of Management and Budget (OMB) reviews the final rule before release.
  • Congress: the committees that oversee Medicare (House Ways and Means, House Energy and Commerce, Senate Finance) hold hearings, and lawmakers send comment letters. The final rule is also sent to Congress and GAO under the Congressional Review Act. Major rules normally wait 60 days before taking effect, but CMS has cited good cause to waive that delay so the new rates can start January 1.

The final rule is usually released around the start of November and published in the Federal Register a few days later.

Step 8: New rates take effect on January 1

Every claim is paid using the payment formula from Part 1 with the new RVUs, GPCIs and conversion factor. Medicare pays 80%; the patient or their supplemental insurance pays 20% after the deductible.

Worked example with toy numbers:

Part RVUs × GPCI =
Work 1.00 1.00 1.00
Practice expense 0.80 1.10 0.88
Malpractice 0.20 0.60 0.12
Adjusted RVUs 2.00
× Conversion factor $29.90
Fee schedule amount $59.80

Medicare pays 80% ($47.84); the patient pays 20% ($11.96).

If Congress acts late

Congress can pass a law changing the conversion factor at any point, often in late December, after the final rule is out. When that happens:

  1. CMS recalculates the conversion factor to apply the change the law requires.
  2. CMS republishes the payment amount for every code, since each one is RVUs × GPCI × conversion factor.
  3. Medicare's claims processors load the new rates.

RVUs and GPCIs usually stay as finalized, unless the same law also changes a payment policy.

Part 4: Summary

  1. Codes enter the annual update: new, revised or flagged as possibly misvalued.
  2. The RUC recommends work RVUs and direct PE inputs, based on specialty surveys.
  3. CMS decides all RVUs: it accepts, changes or replaces RUC recommendations, prices PE inputs, and uses premium data for malpractice. GPCIs adjust each part for local costs.
  4. PE and malpractice are balanced within their own pools: every code is scaled so that each pool's total (RVUs × fixed prior-year billing counts) stays the same.
  5. The conversion factor absorbs work RVU changes through budget neutrality, then applies the changes set by law (the annual update, plus any temporary changes added or expiring). There are separate conversion factors for qualifying APM participants and for anesthesia.
  6. Proposed rule, comments, final rule: Steps 4 and 5 are recalculated after final RVU changes.
  7. January 1: payment = location-adjusted RVUs × conversion factor. If Congress changes the conversion factor late, CMS recalculates and republishes the rates.
